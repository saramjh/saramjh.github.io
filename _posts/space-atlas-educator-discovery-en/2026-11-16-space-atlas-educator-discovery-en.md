## The software behind the two links

The [Moon Phases model](https://spaceatlasedu.org/moon/phases/) connects two ways of seeing one event: a system view of the Sun, Earth, and Moon, and a view of the Moon's illuminated face from Earth. Change the Moon's position in the model, and the visible phase changes with it.

That link between the controls and the two visual explanations is the engineering work I wanted to make useful to educators. The model simplifies scale, body sizes, and orbital inclination to explain a geometric idea. It isn't designed for precise ephemeris calculations or eclipse forecasts.

NCASE's listing groups Space Atlas with other general education resources and describes interactive models for Moon phases, seasons, and astronomical scale. Having a source link on that page makes the project discoverable through an independent education directory. The public listing doesn't disclose how many educators used it, and it doesn't establish an institutional partnership.

## Making a simulator useful in a lesson

A simulation alone doesn't tell a teacher what students should investigate. The two-page [Share My Lesson worksheet](https://sharemylesson.com/teaching-resource/moon-phases-predict-test-explain-interactive-grades-4-8-lesson-412086), titled *Moon Phases: Predict, Test, Explain*, turns the interaction into a specific Grades 4–8 exercise.

1. **Predict.** Sketch how much of the Moon looks lit at New, First Quarter, Full, and Last Quarter.
2. **Test.** Select those four positions in the simulator and compare the top-down Sun–Earth–Moon arrangement with the view from Earth.
3. **Explain.** Use the comparison to explain why Earth's shadow isn't responsible for ordinary monthly lunar phases.

The worksheet includes teacher guidance, an investigation table, a discussion prompt, and notes on the model's simplified geometry. Its suggested **20–25 minutes** is a planning estimate in the published resource, not a measured average from real classrooms. The public resource description is readable without an account; downloading the PDF may require a Share My Lesson login.

For me, the technical lesson was about **turning an interactive control into an assessable question**. The same system view and Earth view that make the simulator work also support prediction, observation, and explanation.

## What an educator should check

The relevant original pages are [NCASE's listing](https://ncase.org/programs/), the [Share My Lesson resource](https://sharemylesson.com/teaching-resource/moon-phases-predict-test-explain-interactive-grades-4-8-lesson-412086), and the [live model](https://spaceatlasedu.org/moon/phases/). Share My Lesson labels the downloadable worksheet **CC BY-NC-ND**. That license describes the worksheet, not the simulator software or third-party content.

The resource description also discloses analytics and advertising on the model's hosting site. Before using the tool with children, educators must independently assess their school rules for privacy, accessibility, consent, and devices. No independent child-privacy certification is claimed.

What I can verify today is a public external directory entry and an author-published teaching resource. Neither link proves school adoption, learning gains, endorsement, or visitor growth.
